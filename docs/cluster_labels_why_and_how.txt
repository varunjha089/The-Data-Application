WHY THE CITESPACE LABELS WERE NOT USED
--------------------------------------
CiteSpace names a cluster with one term chosen by a statistical score (LSI, LLR or MI) from the text of the documents behind the nodes.
That works for keyword or reference networks. In a network whose nodes are COUNTRIES it gives labels that describe one dominant member or an
accident of the text, not the group:
  - cluster 6 (Brazil, Japan, Mexico, Colombia, Peru...) was "random forest regression"; cluster 8 (Egypt, Pakistan, Thailand...) was "sensitivity models";
    cluster 9 was "time-series": these are methods words that many papers share, they do not say what the group is about.
  - cluster 0 (China, United States, India...) was "hyperspectral data" although hyperspectral work is only a small part of its papers.
A reader of a country network wants to know WHO is in the group (geography or collaboration pattern) and WHAT it works on (theme).
So every name has the form "<who>: <what>", or only the theme when the countries do not share a region.

HOW THE NAMES WERE CHOSEN (steps that can be repeated on any CiteSpace country network)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. Members. Take the cluster of every node from CiteSpace's network_summary_table.csv (column ClusterID, column Label = the country).
   Leave out nodes that are not countries (here 11 names such as TOAN QUANG, XIULI, EQUAL CONTRIBUTOR: parsing errors of the converted file).
   Match the names to the country names of the cleaned data (VIET NAM = Vietnam).
2. Papers of the cluster. Take every paper of the cleaned data that has at least one member country (Countries column).
   A paper can belong to several clusters, as it does in the network.
3. What is special about those papers. For every author keyword compute the log-odds ratio of being in the cluster's papers against all other papers:
       score = ln( (a + 0.5) / (n - a + 0.5) ) - ln( (b + 0.5) / (N - n - b + 0.5) )
   a = papers of the cluster with the keyword, n = papers of the cluster, b = papers outside the cluster with the keyword, N = all papers.
   Keep keywords with at least 3 papers (at least 2 when the cluster has fewer than 60 papers). Read the 8 best.
   The log-odds ratio favours keywords that are frequent in the cluster and rare elsewhere, so general words (remote sensing, water quality)
   do not win, but a keyword that occurs only twice can still rank high: read the list, do not take it blindly.
4. Name. Geography from the members with most papers (and the region of the others), theme from the distinguishing keywords
   (instruments and data: altimetry, Sentinel-1/3, Landsat, C2RCC; tasks: flood mapping, water monitoring; objects: lakes, reservoirs, phytoplankton).
   Rules: at most 6 words, a descriptive phrase (not one method word), same style for all clusters, plain English, no abbreviation unless it is the
   usual name of the thing (NDWI, GIS, IoT).
5. Strength of the evidence. strong = at least 3 supporting keywords with at least 3 papers each; medium = 2 to 3 supporting keywords;
   weak = the cluster is small (fewer than 50 papers), so the keywords rest on 2 to 4 papers.
6. Check. Read the title of a few papers per cluster, and the cluster's top countries in the narrative summary, before the name goes into a manuscript.

LIMITS
------
- The clusters come from the collaboration links, not from the themes: countries that work together do not always work on the same topic.
  That is why some clusters have a clear theme (1, 2, 4) and others only a weak one (5, 7, 9).
- Keywords of the papers of a cluster include papers written with countries of other clusters: the evidence shows what the cluster's countries
  publish, not what only they publish.
- The names were first chosen (2026-10-23) with only the top-cited countries of each cluster; the check with the full member lists above gave the same
  best keywords, so no name was changed.
- The ranking of keywords depends on the data: on another data set run the steps again; do not reuse these names.

FOR THE APPLICATION (idea, not built)
-------------------------------------
A task could read CiteSpace's network_summary_table.csv and the cleaned data, remove non-country nodes, and write for each cluster: members, number of
papers, mean year, the 8 distinguishing author keywords (steps 1 to 3) and the strength (step 5), as a table the user can read when naming the clusters.
The final name stays the user's decision (steps 4 and 6).
